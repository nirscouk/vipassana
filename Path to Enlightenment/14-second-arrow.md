# Chapter 14 · Sensation, craving, and the art of not adding a second arrow

The Buddha spoke of two arrows. The first is the unavoidable: heat, loss, a harsh word, a dying body. The second is the one we fire ourselves: this should not be; I cannot bear this; I must have the opposite. Vipassanā is training to feel the first arrow completely, and to put the bow down.

*Vedanā* in *vedanā*: feel the feeling directly. No second object. Pleasant, unpleasant, or neither. Gross or subtle, settled or unsettled. Anything that arises in the mind spreads into the body. That meeting place is where craving is born, and where it can die.

How do you join *anicca* to equanimity? Check the sensation as it is, and appreciate its law. Not as a motto. As a demonstration the body is already giving.

*Saṅkhāra* means the patterns of reaction, the grooves in sensation. Some are like a line in water — gone as they are drawn. Some are like a line in sand. Some are cut in stone. Old fear, old lust, old pride: stone-work. Each equanimous moment is weather on that stone. You will not finish the mountain in a week. You can stop carving new letters today.

*Udaya* and *vaya* — arising and passing — if only understood, become mere awareness. Mere awareness leaves the deep grooves untouched. Those require equanimity and clear knowing of rise and fall. Then dissolution can come, and with it layer after layer of the past. Let them come. Do not make a self out of them. Do not make a self out of being the one who is dissolving.

### Why *anicca* at the level of sensation is the hinge

The mind does not usually react to the outer event. It reacts to the sensation the event lights up in the body. Someone insults you. First there is heat in the chest, a racing heart. Then the mind hates *that feeling*, and calls the hate anger. Left untrained, the deep mind treats an unpleasant sensation as if it will last forever — so it panics, fights, or flees. It treats a pleasant sensation as if it must be kept — so it clings. Scanning the body and knowing “this too is changing,” without adding a reaction, is how the habit loop is not written again.

A simple map:

```
Outer event  →  bodily sensation  →  old loop: fear-centre grabs, story, reaction
                                  →  new loop: stay, know it is changing, the alarm dies down
```

### What a stiller science is beginning to describe

This is not a proof of Nibbāna. It is a description of why the method is not magic.

The brain’s reading of the inner body — heartbeat, breath, tension — is called interoception. Deep body-awareness practice is linked with stronger work in the insula, the region that maps those inner signals. You feel the stress *as physics* before it becomes a whole weather system called “my day is ruined.”

Discomfort on the cushion normally wakes the amygdala, the alarm. Collected attention appears to strengthen the quieter conversation between the prefrontal cortex — the part that can wait and decide — and that alarm. The volume knob turns down when the body is known as temporary, not as a threat.

Psychology calls the same move extinction, or urge-surfing: feel the craving or the pain, wait, let it peak and fall like a wave, do not act. Each time you do this, the old pairing “discomfort means consume or flee” loosens.

Pain also feeds a story-maker in the mind: *Why is my back doing this? Have I injured myself? This sitting is wasted.* That commentary is a second fire. Tracking *anicca* keeps you at the raw data — heat, pulse, pressure — and severs the story from the sensation. The first arrow may remain. The second often does not.

**Today.** When pleasant feeling appears — praise, sugar, a good sitting — notice the lean of the mind toward more. When unpleasant feeling appears, notice the lean toward away. Name the lean. Stay with the sensation until it changes on its own. You have just practised the Third Noble Truth in miniature.
